The matching statistics of a query string $q$ with respect to a
reference string $r$ is the array of lengths of longest substrings in
$q$ that match somewhere in $r$ \cite[p. 132ff]{gus97:alg}. Consider
for example $q=\ty{AAAATATTAA}$ and $r=\ty{TTAAAATTAA}$. The first
matching statistic, $\ms[1]$, is the length of the longest prefix of
$q[1...]$ that matches somewhere in $r$. This happens to
be \ty{AAAAT}, which matches at $r[3]$, so $\ms[1]=5$. The next match
statistic, $\ms[2]=4$, and so on, as shown in Table~\ref{tab:ms1}.

In our program Fur, we calculate something similar to the matching
statistics, which we call the match lengths, $\ml$. Instead of matches
starting at every position in $q$, we find a match of length $\ell$
starting at $q[i]$, store $\ell$ in $\ml[i]$, and skip the match plus
its flanking mismatch before starting the next match at
$q[i+\ell+1]$. These matching steps are summarized in lines 3--7 of
Algorithm~\ref{alg:ml}. After this matching phase, we interpolate the
missing match lengths as $\ml[i]\leftarrow \ml[i-1]-1$ wherever
$\ml[i] < \ml[i-1]-1$ (lines 8--12).

Calculating $\ml$ doesn't require suffix links, which makes it easier
than calculating $\ms$. However, $\ml$ is at best an approximation of
$\ms$. In Table~\ref{tab:ms1} $\ms$ and $\ml$ differ at positions 5
and 6, in Table~\ref{tab:ms2} at five, positions 2, 3, 4, 7, and 8.

The point of calculating $\ml$ is to distinguish regions in $q$ that
are homologous to $r$ from regions that are not. The question is,
whether $\ms$ and $\ml$ are equivalent for this purpose. The
program \ty{mast} is intended for easy calculation of $\ms$ and $\ml$
to answer that question. Take a look at the Tutorial for a comparison
of $\ms$ and $\ml$ on simulated data.
